About

I hold an MA in Political Science and International Relations from Nazarbayev University (2025, graduated with honors). My quantitative thesis surveyed 235 Kazakhstani students on public service careers and ran the first Central Asian application of the international public service motivation scale.

I work as a technical writer at an AI company, where I document agentic AI systems in production. Spending my days inside these systems is what convinced me they deserve study from the citizen's side of the counter.

Research

Kazakhstan adopted the first comprehensive AI law in Central Asia in late 2025. Citizens now hold a legal right to know when an algorithm shaped a decision about them, to demand an explanation, and to object. Whether anyone trusts the algorithmic state enough to use those rights is an open question, and the behavioral literature cannot answer it: algorithm aversion research was built almost entirely on Western samples, where the human official is the trusted default.

In much of the post-Soviet space the default is different. The human at the counter has long been associated with petty corruption, and digital services earned public support precisely by removing that human. My research programme tests whether this context inverts the Western canon, and what it means when a state can borrow legitimacy from the machines it deploys.

The empirical core is a pair of preregistered studies in Kazakhstan: a survey experiment on how citizens judge unfavourable government decisions made by officials, algorithms, and the hybrid the state actually runs, and a performance-based study of whether the officials who sign algorithmic recommendations can detect errors in them.
